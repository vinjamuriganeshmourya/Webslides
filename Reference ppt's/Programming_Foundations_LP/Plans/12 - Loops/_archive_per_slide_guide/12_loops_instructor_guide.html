Instructor guide

One entry per slide, in the order you will present them. Screen only, it is not designed to print.

Checkpoint 1: they can name one machine that repeats one job after a single press. If not, take the examples in the hook down to two and move on.

Opening · 1 min · card 1 of 32

Welcome, while they settle

How it goes
  1. This is up as they walk in and find seats. Say nothing over it.
  2. When the room is quiet, one line: “good morning, laptops shut, please. I will tell you when to open them.”
  • Ten seconds. Move on as soon as the talking stops.
  • Do not name the topic here. They guess it later, and the card after the hook tells them.

Watch for Nothing. This card is the room settling.

Recap · 3 min · card 3 of 32

Find the line that lies

How it goes
  1. Read the program out, line by line, with the line numbers. Marks is 30.
  2. Say what it was meant to do: “Pass should only print when marks is 35 or more. Marks is 30. So this should print nothing at all.”
  3. Then the task, and then silence: “it prints Pass. One line is to blame. Decide which one.” Twenty seconds. Do not talk over it. Laptops are still shut and the worksheet is not out yet, so this one is fingers, not writing.
  4. Take the vote: “hold up that many fingers, all together, on three.” Then count the room out loud, number by number.
  5. Press Show me. Press 1 puts the wrong output up; press 2 lights line 5 and says why; press 3 shows what it should have printed.
  6. Close on the idea today needs: “the indent decides which block a line belongs to. Line 5 is at the left edge, so it is outside both ifs.”
  • Short of time: press straight to 3 and read the green line. The vote is what costs the minute.
  • The panel drives itself: Show me steps it, Replay puts it back. Arrow keys work inside it too.
  • Hands down before you press. A recap they watch teaches nothing.
  • Do not reteach nesting. Three presses, three minutes, move.

Watch for A room that votes for line 3 has the nesting right but not the indent. Say the rule once and carry on; it comes back all session.

Hook · 6 min · card 4 of 32

Forty copies, and where else they have seen it

How it goes
  1. Press 1 is up: the shop, and nothing inside the machine yet. Start from something all of them have done: “the xerox shop outside the gate. You hand over one form and say forty copies.”
  2. Say what they already know, so nobody has to correct you: “he does not press the button forty times. He types 40, presses START once, and goes back to his phone.”
  3. Now the question that is actually interesting: “but the machine only knows how to make one copy. So what is happening inside it, forty times over?” Take two or three answers. Take the wrong ones too. Correct nobody.
  4. Press 2. Only now does the column of forty jobs appear inside the machine. Let them look at it: “forty of them. One for every copy.”
  5. Press 3. The note: “one job, forty times over. That is all the machine is doing.” Ask before you move on: “and if he had typed four hundred?” Wait for the groan.
  6. Press 4. The machine goes and the question changes: where else have you seen this? Then stop talking. Nudge instead: “anything on this campus with a button on it.”
  7. Take three or four. Make each one say both halves: the machine, and the one job it repeats. “ATM” is half an answer; “the ATM counts one note, again and again, until it reaches the amount” is the answer.
  8. Presses 5, 6, 7. One of ours per press, only after theirs. The three empty boxes are up from press 4, so the room can see how many are coming. Tie each to whichever student answer it matches.
  9. Press 8. Back to the machine, and the column collapses onto one line. Say nothing at all while it moves. Then: “one instruction, written once. Forty copies still come out.”
  10. Point at the code and say plainly what it is, without naming it: “this is what is running behind the scenes in that machine. What it is called, and how to write one, that is today.”
  • Short of time: take two student examples, not four, and show only the ATM before press 8.
  • Eight presses. Arrow keys step it in presenter view; Replay puts it back to press 1.
  • Press 1 is the shop and the question, and nothing inside the machine. Do not press on until you have taken two or three answers: press 2 is the answer to what you just asked.
  • No timer on this card. A clock turns a call for examples into a task with a right answer.
  • Do not claim the operator presses the button forty times. They know he does not, and the hook dies the moment one of them says so.
  • Never go round naming people. Keep their answers on the board; they come back at the takeaways.

Watch for If somebody says “loop” before you do, take it and use their word all session.

Concept · 50 sec · card 7 of 32 · in an 3 min block

Every line runs once, and you can watch it

How it goes
  1. Ask it before you press anything: “so far, when Python reaches a line, how many times does it run that line?” Wait. Take “once” from the room.
  2. Presses 1 to 5. One press per line. Say the line number and what it did, nothing more: “line 1, a is 5. Line 2, a is 6, and the old 5 is struck out. Line 3 prints it.”
  3. On press 4 point at the box: “a is 7 now. Same two lines as before, written out again.” That is the sentence the worksheet task is about to charge them for.
  4. Press 6. The verdict, then the question under it. Read the question once, slowly, and do not answer it: “before I answer that, I want you to feel why we need it.”
  • Short of time: press straight to 6 and read the two bands. The walk is what costs the minute.
  • Six presses. Arrow keys step it; Replay puts it back to line 1.
  • Fifty seconds. It exists to make the next task feel unreasonable.
  • Do not say the word loop on this card. They have not been given it yet.

Watch for Somebody will say “write it ten times”. Good, that is exactly what the worksheet is about to ask them to do.

Worksheet 1 · 3 min · card 9 of 32 · worksheet task 1

Ten numbers, the long way

How it goes

  2. Read the change once: “same question, but now it asks for the next ten.”
  3. Say the two things they write: the lines they would add, and then the two numbers in the little table.

Concept · 60 sec · card 10 of 32 · in an 4 min block

A loop runs a block several times

How it goes
  1. Open from their own sheets: “twenty-one lines, and you all wrote the same two of them ten times.”
  2. Now name it, pointing at the orange arrow: “a loop lets us execute one block of code several times.”
  3. Check them with a question, do not tell: “how many different lines are in that blue block? One block, how many runs?”
  • Do not name while or for yet. The next card does that, and it lands better as an answer.
  • Sixty seconds.

Watch for If the room is quiet at the check question, narrow it: “point at the block that repeats.”

Concept · 20 sec · card 11 of 32 · in an 4 min block

Python has two ways to loop

How it goes

  2. Read it plainly: two ways, the while loop and the for loop.
  3. Say which is which and when: “today is while, start to finish. The for loop comes in a later session. Both do the same job; they are written differently.”

Concept · 60 sec · card 12 of 32 · in an 4 min block

The while shape

How it goes
  1. Straight on: “this is the first one, and it is called while.”
  2. Read the shape off the picture in order, the word while, the condition, the colon, and then the indented block underneath it.
  3. Connect it to yesterday out loud: “same colon, same indent as an if. The difference is one word.”
  4. Then the difference that matters: “an if asks its condition once. This one asks it again, every time round.”
  • Point at the indent with your hand. That is the same rule they learned yesterday.
  • Sixty seconds. The build that follows is where it gets assembled.

Watch for A student saying “so it is an if that repeats” has it. Use their sentence.

Checkpoint 3: they can name the three parts on any loop. If not, run the dot a second time at 2 and drop the square pattern to three minutes.

The build · 6 min · card 15 of 32 · in an 8 min block

Build it, name it, count it: seven presses

How it goes
  1. Press 1. The skeleton is up: the input line, the two lines from their own sheet that repeat, and a while line with a gap where the condition should be. Say what is missing: “three lines to go, and you are going to give me one of them.”
  2. Press 2: initialization. Ask before you press: “if I want this to happen exactly three times, what do I need first?” Take “a count” from the room, then press.
  3. Read the card: it runs once. Push it: “even if the loop goes round a hundred times?” Still once. Write the word on the board.
  4. Press 3: updation. Ask first: “the counter is sitting at zero. Who is going to change it?” Press. The question that matters is where: “is that line inside the loop or outside? How can you tell?” Wait for the indent. Write the word.
  5. Press 4: the question. A red dashed box, and nothing else. Read it out and then stop talking. Ten seconds of silence.
  6. Then take answers from three different parts of the room before you say whether any is right. Push on what they give you: “counter equals three? When would that be True? Walk it with me.”
  7. Press 5: termination condition. The answer lands in the gap. Check it against their answers, not against the screen. Now the count, and ask before you read it: “the body runs three times. How many times does line 3 run?” Most of the room will say three.
  8. Let them be wrong, then count it together on the board: 0 < 3 yes, 1 < 3 yes, 2 < 3 yes, 3 < 3 no. Four. “Three yeses and one no. The no is how it stops.” Write the words on the board.
  9. Press 6: block of code. Three times, only while the answer is True. Thirty seconds; they can see it.
  10. Press 7: run it. Input 5 gives 6, 7, 8, End. Close on it: “four parts. Three of them you write yourself, and the fourth is the work you actually wanted done.”
  • Short of time: shorten presses 6 and 7. Never the silence on press 4 or the count on press 5.
  • Seven presses, and this one card replaces what used to be five slides. Do not rush it, it is the centre of the session.
  • Presses 4 and 5 are the whole thing. If you are running late, shorten presses 6 and 7, never the silence on press 4 or the count on press 5.
  • Write initialization, termination condition and updation on the board as they arrive, and leave all three there for the rest of the session.

Watch for A student who says “four” before you count it has understood the back edge already. Ask them to say why, and let the room hear it from them rather than from you.

Concept · 60 sec · card 16 of 32 · in an 8 min block

The shape, with the names on it

How it goes

  2. Read it top to bottom against the words already on your board: initialization outside, termination condition on the while line, block of code indented, updation last and still indented.

Worksheet 2 · 4 min · card 17 of 32 · worksheet task 2

Which part is this line?

How it goes
  1. Worksheet task 2. Two programs, and they have not seen either of them.
  2. Say the task once: “six lines. Against each one type I, T or U. I for initialization, T for termination condition, U for updation.” Say the words in full, not the letters only.
  3. Do Program A line 2 together, out loud, so nobody is guessing at the task.
  4. Press play. Two and a half minutes. Walk the rows, and keep talking to them: “any doubts about the task? Ask now”, “one minute, and the question underneath”.
  5. Hands off keyboards first. Then say the check, not before: every loop has exactly one of each. If you have typed the same letter three times, look again.
  6. Now the panel. Take the first line, count hands for I, for T, for U, and tap the bin the room voted for. If it snaps home, read the green line. If it bounces, read the red line and take the vote again.
  7. Program B is where it gets interesting. Both of its wrong bins are real beliefs: that initialization always sets zero, and that updation always adds one. Let those bounce.
  8. Last thing, and do not skip it: hands for the count on line 4 of Program A. Take 5, and take 6. Then walk it out loud: counter is 1 yes, 2 yes, 3 yes, 4 yes, 5 yes, 6 no. Five passes, six questions. “Same shape as the four you counted on the build card.”
  • Short of time: tap the first four lines and say the last two rather than taking a vote on them.
  • Press play on the timer before they start typing.
  • Tap the bin the room voted for, not the right one. A bin that bounces is the whole point of the panel.
  • Replay clears the bins if you want to run a line again.
  • Say the self-check only after they have stopped typing. On the worksheet it would be a hint, not a check.

Watch for Program B line 2 and line 5 are the two that bounce most. If they both snap home first time, the three parts have landed and you can take a minute off the reveals later. The six answers are I, T, U for Program A lines 2, 3 and 6, and I, T, U for Program B lines 2, 3 and 5. Line 4 of Program A runs five times: counter starts at 1 and the test is less than or equal to 5, so count the yeses.

Explainer · 3 min · card 18 of 32

Ask it again: follow the dot

How it goes
  1. Say this, do not look for it on the card. “The red dot is Python. I am going to set the number, and before I press play I want you to tell the person next to you what this prints.” The card carries the drawing only; this sentence is yours.
  2. Open from the lines they just named: “you found the three lines. Now watch what Python does with them.”
  3. The slider is set to 2. Before you touch anything: “tell the person next to you what this prints. All of it.” Ten seconds, out loud, then quiet.
  4. Press Play, then narrate the dot as it moves, one step per sentence, asking rather than telling: “first it sets a to 5, and counter to 0. Now it reaches the diamond. Is 0 less than 2? Yes, so we go inside …”
  5. Then, with your hand on the back edge: “look where it goes next. It runs back up that line, to the same question. Is 1 less than 2? Yes again.”
  6. On the pass that fails, slow right down: “counter is 2. Is 2 less than 2? No. So we do not go in. We come out at the bottom and print End.”
  7. Tie it back to the build: “count the times the dot touched that diamond. Three yeses and a no, four. Same number as before.”
  8. Now drag the slider to 0 and ask before playing: “what prints now?” Take the vote. Most of the room says 6. Play it: the whole body greys out and stays grey.
  • Short of time: run it once at 2 and once at 0, and skip the narration in between.
  • One step is the button to use while you narrate, it stops on the line they argued about.
  • Press Reset before you leave the card, or the next class sees a finished run.
  • Three minutes. If you are behind, run it once at 2 and once at 0 and skip the narration in between.

Watch for The 0 prediction is the one that splits the room. Let them be wrong out loud before you play it.

Quiz · 6 min · card 19 of 32

Classroom quiz, on the portal

How it goes
  1. Card up, and announce it: “open the learning portal on your laptop. Today's classroom quiz is live.”
  2. Say the two things they need: how long they have, and that it is individual. Five minutes, own laptop, no talking.
  3. Do not read any question out, and do not put one on the board. The portal has them.

  • A student who finishes early goes back to worksheet task 2 and checks their line numbers.
  • Five to six minutes total, including the two sentences at the end.

Watch for The quiz is the checkpoint for the whole first half. A weak result here is not a reason to reteach from the start; it is a reason to slow the reveals down.

Checkpoint 4: they can say why a loop never stops. If not, say reveal 4 in one sentence rather than putting the card up.

Hands-on · 6 min · card 20 of 32

Type it, then break it

How it goes
  1. Laptops open. “Type this exactly. Do not copy mine, type it.” The first line asks for a, so run it with a = 5, then a = 1, then a = 100. Three minutes.
  2. Walk the rows. Most errors will be the indent or the colon. Say so out loud once so nobody feels singled out.
  3. Step to the second panel and frame change 1 as your own mistake: “I think I have left a line out by accident, delete counter equals counter plus one and run it again with a = 5.” Do not warn them, and cover change 2 with your hand if you have to.
  4. Let them react. Somebody will shout. Only then: “what is it waiting for? Counter is still zero, so the answer to the question never changes.”
  5. Show them the stop button once. Then, on the same card, the second change: put the line back, and move it above print(a).
  6. Take the difference from the room: “same three numbers? No, what changed?”
  • Short of time: take change 1 only. It is the one the four common mistakes card comes back to.
  • Two panels, not three: change 1 and change 2 are on one card, with one timer for both. Read change 1 out, wait, then point at change 2. Do not let them do both at once.
  • Know where the stop button is in your playground before the session.
  • They meet the fourth common mistake here by doing it. The card that names it comes after the worksheet hunt, not before.

Watch for Moving the update above print(a) prints 6, 7, 8 still, counter is not printed. The lesson is that order inside the body matters for what prints, not for how many passes.

Worksheet 3 · 4 min · card 21 of 32 · worksheet task 3

Find the mistake

How it goes
  1. Open from what they just did: “you broke updation on purpose. Here are three programs somebody broke by accident.”
  2. Worksheet task 3. Say the shape of it once: three programs, one thing wrong in each.
  3. Say the three things they type: which line is wrong, or, if something is simply missing, what is missing and where it should go. Then which part it is, then what happens when you run it. Read the inputs off the sheet: 5, then 10, then 1.
  4. Press play. Three minutes. Worksheet tab only, say why: running them answers the question for free.
  5. Walk the rows. “Which part is that? Say the word” · “program 3 is the sneaky one, count the yeses”.
  6. Hands off keyboards. Do not take the answers here, the next four cards are the answers, one at a time.
  • Short of time: two minutes on the timer, and say program 3 is the one to look at twice.
  • Press play on the timer.
  • Program 1 is the one where no line is wrong. That is deliberate, and it is why the task also asks what is missing.
  • Two of the three are the initialization: program 1 has none at all, and program 3 has one with the wrong value in it. Do not claim they are three different parts, because they are not. Say it at the reveal instead: the same part can be missing or simply wrong.
  • Worksheet tab only. If somebody runs program 2 they freeze a tab, and you lose the reveal.
  • Answers, for you only: program 1 has no initialization; program 2 stores the answer of the condition in a box; program 3 starts the counter at 1 and runs twice, not three times.

Watch for Program 3 is the one most of them will mark correct. That is the finding, and it is worth saying out loud when the reveal comes.

Reveal 1 of 4 · 60 sec · card 22 of 32 · in an 4 min block

Missing initialization

How it goes
  1. Program 1 first. Ask for hands before you show anything: “who typed that a line is missing?”
  2. Then reveal, and read the error text out exactly: name counter is not defined.
  3. Ask them to name the part: “which of the three is missing?” Take the word from the room, not from you.
  4. Say the rule they can use in an exam: “if you ever see that error under a while loop, look above the loop, not inside it.”
  5. One extra beat worth twenty seconds: “notice it did not loop forever. It did not loop at all, it stopped before the first pass.”
  • Short of time: read the error text and move on.
  • Sixty seconds. It is the easy one of the four.

Watch for Students often read NameError as a typo. Say plainly it is not spelling, the line that creates the variable is simply not there.

Reveal 2 of 4 · 70 sec · card 23 of 32 · in an 4 min block

A condition stored in a box

How it goes
  1. Program 2. Hands first: “who said line 3? Who said line 4?” Both are defensible, so say so.
  2. Read line 3 aloud very slowly: condition equals, bracket, counter less than three, bracket.
  3. Ask the one question and then stop talking: “what gets stored in condition, the question, or the answer?”
  4. Now the card: the answer, True, stored once. Line 4 asks the box, not the counter. The last line keeps changing counter, and nothing ever looks at counter again.
  5. Name it: infinite loop. Then connect it back: “same ending as when you deleted the update line. Different cause.”
  • Seventy seconds. This is the hard one of the four.
  • Call the variable “the box called condition” every time, or the sentence becomes unparseable.

Watch for Two different causes, one symptom. If a student conflates them, ask: “which line was wrong in each?”

Reveal 3 of 4 · 70 sec · card 24 of 32 · in an 4 min block

Off by one

How it goes
  1. Program 3, and ask the honest question: “how many of you marked this one correct?” Count the hands. It will be most of the room.
  2. Point at the output: input is 1, three numbers were wanted, two came out.
  3. Walk the counter out loud with them: starts at 1, so 1 yes, 2 yes, 3 no. Two passes.
  4. Give them both repairs: start at 0 and keep less than 3, or start at 1 and use less than 4. Ask which they prefer and why.
  • Short of time: take the count of hands and give one repair, not both.
  • This is the mistake that will cost them marks in an exam. Say that out loud.
  • The deck heads this card Incorrect Termination Condition and the reveal before it as well. Say why both names are defensible: the counter starts in the wrong place, so you can repair it at the start or at the test, and there is nothing to choose between them.
  • Seventy seconds, and the count of hands at the start is the point of it.

Watch for Anyone who says “just change it to 4” is right and should be asked to say why it works, that is the understanding you want.

Reveal 4 of 4 · 50 sec · card 25 of 32 · in an 4 min block

Not updating the counter

  3. Close the whole set: “initialization, termination condition, updation. You have now broken all three and seen what each one costs, an error before it starts, the wrong number of passes, and a loop that never stops.”

  • Fifty seconds. It is a naming card, not a new idea, they did the discovery already.
  • If you are short of time, this is the one reveal you can say in a sentence without putting the card up.

Concept · 90 sec · card 26 of 32 · in an 8 min block

The square pattern

How it goes
  1. Read the question off the card: stars in N rows and N columns.
  2. Point at the output and say the thing that costs marks: there is a space after every star.
  3. Give them the shortcut they already own: “which string operation gives you a star and a space, n times?” Take string repetition from the room.
  4. Ask, and hold the answer: “which part repeats, the row, or the star?” They decide it at the keyboard.
  • Short of time: say the space rule and hand over. Do not take the row-or-star question.
  • Do not code this on screen. They write it.
  • Say the space rule twice. It is the single most common reason a correct program fails a test case here. If nobody recalls string repetition, write "* " * 3 on the board and let them read it.

Watch for A room that says “both” is ahead of the syllabus, tell them they are right, and that the inner one has a shortcut today.

Hands-on · 5 min · card 27 of 32 · in an 8 min block

Write it in the playground

How it goes
  1. Laptops open, playground open, blank file. Only the input line is on screen; everything after it is theirs.
  2. Before anybody types, take one answer out loud: “n is 3. How many times does the print line have to run?” Three. That is their termination condition, and now they know it.
  3. Press play. Four and a half minutes. Walk the rows continuously, “are you guys done?” · “show me your three parts” · “run it with 5”.
  4. For anyone stuck, ask the three words, do not give code: “where is your initialization?”
  5. Last ninety seconds: everybody runs it with 3, then 5, then 1.
  • Short of time: three minutes on the timer, and run it with 3 only.
  • Press play on the timer.
  • Nothing goes on the worksheet for this one, they type it. The prediction happens out loud before the laptops open.
  • Do not put a solution on screen while they work.

Watch for The common bug is counter less than 3 hard-coded instead of counter less than n, it passes their first test and fails the second. Let them find it with input 5.

Concept · 60 sec · card 28 of 32 · in an 8 min block

The solution

How it goes
  1. Up on screen only after the timer has run out.
  2. Read it against their three words: initialization, termination condition using n, the block, updation.
  3. Point at the line that does the work: print of star-space times n, inside a loop that runs n times. “The star repeats across with the string. The row repeats down with the loop.”
  4. Ask for hands: “who got the same three parts, even if your variable names are different?”
  • Short of time: ask for hands on the three parts and skip the line-by-line read.
  • Do not retype it. Compare it to what is already on their screens.
  • A student who stored the row in a variable first is also correct, say so out loud.

Watch for Anyone whose program works for 4 but not 5 hard-coded the number. Name the symptom, not the student.

Exit · 4 min · card 29 of 32

One question, and it is hard

How it goes
  1. Frame it as a real thing: “a name-board app prints one letter per line. Somebody types a single letter.”
  2. Read the code once, by line number. Then stop talking and press play on the timer. Two and a half minutes, silent.
  3. Vote by hands, option by option. Count every option out loud.
  4. Reveal. Walk it: length is 1, so the question is 0 less than 0, which is False the first time. The body never runs.
  5. Name each wrong option: 1 is “a loop must run at least once”, 3 is “I saw an index and expected an index error”, 4 is “I saw a while and expected forever”.
  6. Tie it to the recap: “first thing this morning you saw a program that should print nothing. Same idea, harder question.”
  • Press play on the timer. It does not start by itself.
  • a[counter] picks the letter at that position. If anyone asks, say that and move on: the answer does not depend on it, because the body never runs.
  • This runs even if everything else is cut. It is the hard floor.

Watch for A big vote for option 1 is the finding worth carrying into the next session: they still believe a loop body always runs once.

Common guidelines

Things true of every slide

Never start a slide cold

Every slide opens from something the room already has, the worksheet on their screen, the program they just ran, the answer somebody just gave. If a slide feels abrupt, the fix is one sentence at the end of the previous slide, not a longer explanation on this one.

Walk it in steps, and check as you go

Do not deliver a finished explanation. Say one step, then ask the next as a question “so counter is 3 now. Is 3 less than 3? No, right? Good.” If the room does not answer, you have found the gap for free. Offer doubts out loud, often; it costs four seconds.

How every poll runs

Let them read and think first. Then count the hands option by option, out loud, including the options nobody picked. Say once, at the start of the session, that a closed fist means “not sure” and that it is a real answer you want to see. Reveal. Then explain every option, not just the right one, the wrong ones are where the misconceptions live.

Picking students

Call a row or a side of the room, never a name. Rotate which group you call. Never name a student who got something wrong, and never say whose screen you are looking at.

When nobody answers

Count ten seconds in your head. Then narrow the question rather than answering it: “point at the line that repeats” is answerable when “what do you notice?” is not.

Why they commit before they run

It was never really about where they write it down, it is that a student has to commit to an answer before the machine hands them one. The worksheet is where they commit; the playground is where they find out. A student who predicts and then runs it discovers they were wrong; a student who runs it first discovers nothing, because the machine answered before they had a position. So: worksheet tab only on a worksheet beat, and the playground shut. The one beat that has no worksheet task is the square pattern, and there the prediction is taken out loud before any playground opens.

Say the three words in full, every time

Initialization, termination condition, updation. Not “the first bit”, not “the check”, not “the plus one”. They are named once, properly, on the build card. Everything after that, the sheet, the mistake hunt and the map, assumes the room has the words. Write all three on the board as they arrive and leave them there.

The counts are the understanding

For the program on the build card, initialization runs once, the condition is asked four times, and the block and the updation run three times each. The four is the number that tells you whether a student has understood the back edge. Ask for it more than once: on press 5 of the build, again on the dot, again if it comes up on the sheet.

Two words that mean different things today

Say “the termination condition” for the test on the while line, and “the counter” for the variable. Never say “the value” for either. In program 2 of the mistake hunt there is also a variable named condition, call that one “the box called condition” every time, or the sentence becomes unparseable.

Reading the room

Still typing after the timer means the task was too long, take the next one down by a minute. A room on the worksheet tab and nothing else is the beat working. Nobody looking up during a hands-on means they are stuck, not busy; walk and look. A student rerunning the same code unchanged has not read the error. Laughter on the break-it beat is the beat landing.

If you are short of time

Take the mistake hunt down from three minutes of writing to two and go straight to the reveals. Say reveal 4 in a sentence instead of putting the card up. Shorten the square-pattern beat from four and a half minutes to three before you touch anything else. Never drop the exit question, the build card, the classify panel or the dot, and never drop the classroom quiz: it is the portal record for the session.

If they ask

  • “Can I use a for loop instead?” Not today. The for loop has its own session; today the point is the three lines you have to write yourself.
  • “Which is better, while or for?” Neither. You pick by what you are counting, and you will be able to choose once you have both.
  • “Does the counter have to be called counter?” No. Call it i, or count, or anything. The name has no meaning to Python.
  • “Can the counter go down instead of up?” Yes, start at 3 and use counter > 0 with counter = counter - 1. Same three parts.
  • “What if the condition is never False?” The program runs until you stop it. That is the infinite loop, and it is not an error message, nothing is wrong as far as Python is concerned.
  • “Why is print("End") not indented?” Same rule as yesterday: the indent says which block a line belongs to. Outdented means it is outside the loop, so it runs once, after.
  • “My infinite loop froze the playground.” Stop button beside Run. If the tab is unresponsive, reload it, the code is saved.
