Name the session and nothing else

How it goes
  1. Title up: “Loops.”

Recap · 4 min · slide 3 of 27

Recap card, then three questions

How it goes
  1. The card comes up first, and it names the topic: Recap — Nested Conditional Statements. Say it out loud: “last session we learnt about nested conditional statements. Let us do a quick recap of that.”
  2. Press Next. Read Q1 aloud and let them read the code. The timer starts by itself — do not talk over it.
  3. Count hands option by option: “hands for 1… for 2… for 3… for 4.” Say out loud that a closed fist is a real answer you want to see.
  4. Press Reveal. Walk the little trace beside it: blue is what Python ran, faded is what it never even read.
  5. Say why each wrong option is tempting before you move on — 1 is “I assumed both ifs get asked”, 3 is “I lost the outer block”.
  6. Q2 and Q3 the same way, briskly. On Q3 add one line: “remember that — a program that prints nothing.”
  • Next / Back step the card and the three questions. Reveal is a separate press.
  • Do not reteach nesting. Three questions, three minutes, move.

Watch for A big vote for Q2 option 2 means the elif chain has not landed. Note it; do not stop and reteach.

Hook · 4 min · slide 4 of 27

Behind the scenes at the xerox machine

How it goes
  1. Start from something all of them have done: “the xerox shop outside the gate. You hand over one form and say forty copies.”
  2. Say what they already know, so nobody has to correct you: “he does not press the button forty times. He types 40, presses START once, and goes back to his phone.”
  3. Now the question that is actually interesting: “but the machine only knows how to make one copy. So what is happening inside it, forty times over?” Take two or three answers. Take the wrong ones too. Correct nobody.
  4. Press once. The red brace appears: “one job, forty times over. That is all the machine is doing.”
  5. Ask before the last press: “and if he had typed four hundred?” Wait for the groan.
  6. Press again. Say nothing at all while it moves. Then: “one instruction, written once. Forty copies still come out.”
  7. Point at the code and say plainly what it is: “so this kind of code is what is running behind the scenes in that machine. What exactly it is, and how to write one yourself — that is today.” Do not explain the syntax here.
  • Three presses total. Replay if the room missed the collapse — it is worth a second run.
  • Arrow keys step it in presenter view.
  • Do not claim the operator presses the button forty times. They know he does not, and the hook dies the moment one of them says so.

Watch for If somebody says “loop” before you do, take it and use their word all session.

Your turn · 5 min · slide 5 of 27

Where else does one press repeat a job?

How it goes
  1. Carry it straight over from the machine: “one press, and it repeats the same small job until it has done it enough times. Where else have you seen that?”
  2. Then stop talking. The silence is doing the work — do not fill it with your own example. Nudge instead: “think of anything on this campus with a button on it.”
  3. Take three or four. Make each one say both halves: the machine, and the one job it repeats. “ATM” is half an answer; “the ATM counts one note, again and again, until it reaches the amount” is the answer.
  4. Only after they have answered, press Next three times — one example per press. Read each one and tie it to whichever student answer it matches.
  5. Close it: “every one of these is the same shape. One small job, repeated until something is reached.”
  • Three presses, and they come after the room has spoken, not before. If you reveal first, nobody offers anything.
  • Two minutes here. The agenda and the runs-once slide behind it take the other three.
  • Never go round naming people. Keep their answers on the board; they come back at the takeaways.

Watch for Answers that describe one big job rather than one small job repeated. Ask the follow-up: “and how many times does it do that?”

Agenda · 5 min · slide 6 of 27

The four stops, and the sheets

  • Send the worksheet link now and have them download it and open it. This is the only hand-out moment in the session, and everything on paper today is in that one file.

Concept · 5 min · slide 7 of 27

Every block runs once — so far

How it goes
  1. Ask it as a question, do not read the slide: “so far, when Python reaches a line, how many times does it run that line?” Wait. Take “once” from the room.
  2. Point at the picture: top to bottom, each block once. “That is every program you have written so far.”
  3. Now the problem, and leave it open: “so if I need something to happen ten times, what do I do today, with only what you already know?”
  4. Do not answer it. Get the worksheet open instead.
  • Forty seconds. It exists to make the next task feel unreasonable.

Watch for Somebody will say “write it ten times”. Good — that is exactly what the sheet is about to ask them to do.

Paper 1 · 4 min · slide 8 of 27 · worksheet task 1

Ten numbers, the long way

How it goes
  1. Worksheet open, playground shut. Task 1 has the three-number program already in it.
  2. Read the change once: “same question, but now it asks for the next ten.”

  • Press play on the timer. It does not start by itself.
  • Do not show a solution. The next slides are the answer.
  • Task 2 is further down the same file. Tell them to leave it alone for now.

Watch for Twenty-one lines is right (one input line plus ten pairs). Anyone who wrote a loop already has been reading ahead — ask them to hold it for two slides.

Concept · 2 min · slide 9 of 27

A loop runs a block several times

How it goes
  1. Open from their own sheets: “twenty-one lines, and you all wrote the same two of them ten times.”
  2. Now name it, pointing at the orange arrow: “a loop lets us execute one block of code several times.”
  3. Check them with a question, do not tell: “how many different lines are in that blue block? One block, how many runs?”
  • Do not name while or for yet. The next slide does that, and it lands better as an answer.
  • Sixty seconds.

Watch for If the room is quiet at the check question, narrow it: “point at the block that repeats.”

Concept · 2 min · slide 10 of 27

Python has two ways to loop

How it goes
  1. Ask first: “so how do we write one in Python?” Then put the slide up.
  2. Read it plainly: two primary ways — the while loop and the for loop.
  3. Say which is which and when: “today is while, start to finish. The for loop comes in a later session. Both do the same job; they are written differently.”
  4. Do not compare them. One sentence about for, then move.
  • Twenty seconds. Its only job is that nobody leaves thinking while is the only loop there is.
  • Do not promise the for loop for tomorrow — say “a later session” unless you know the timetable.

Watch for Students who have seen for loops elsewhere will want to use one all session. Tell them plainly that today is about the three lines you have to write yourself.

Concept · 2 min · slide 11 of 27

The while shape

How it goes
  1. Straight on: “this is the first one, and it is called while.”
  2. Read the shape off the picture in order — the word while, the condition, the colon, and then the indented block underneath it.
  3. Connect it to yesterday out loud: “same colon, same indent as an if. The difference is one word.”
  4. Then the difference that matters: “an if asks its condition once. This one asks it again, every time round.”
  5. Hand over: “so let us build one, on the question you have already done the long way.”
  • Point at the indent with your hand. That is the same rule they learned yesterday.
  • This is the only slide in the concept run. The build that follows is one card, not four.

Watch for A student saying “so it is an if that repeats” has it. Use their sentence.

Checkpoint 3 — they can name the three parts on any loop

The build · 7 min · slide 12 of 27

Build it, name it, count it — seven presses

How it goes
  1. Press 1. The skeleton is up: the input line, the two lines from their own sheet that repeat, and a while line with a gap where the condition should be. Say what is missing: “three lines to go, and you are going to give me one of them.”
  2. Press 2 — initialization. Ask before you press: “if I want this to happen exactly three times, what do I need first?” Take “a count” from the room, then press.
  3. Read the card: it runs once. Push it: “even if the loop goes round a hundred times?” Still once. Write the word on the board.
  4. Press 3 — updation. Ask first: “the counter is sitting at zero. Who is going to change it?” Press. The question that matters is where: “is that line inside the loop or outside? How can you tell?” Wait for the indent. Write the word.
  5. Press 4 — the question. A red dashed box, and nothing else. Read it out and then stop talking. Ten seconds of silence.
  6. Then take answers from three different parts of the room before you say whether any is right. Push on what they give you: “counter equals three? When would that be True? Walk it with me.”
  7. Press 5 — termination condition. The answer lands in the gap. Check it against their answers, not against the screen. Now the count, and ask before you read it: “the body runs three times. How many times does line 3 run?” Most of the room will say three.
  8. Let them be wrong, then count it together on the board: 0 < 3 yes, 1 < 3 yes, 2 < 3 yes, 3 < 3 no. Four. “Three yeses and one no. The no is how it stops.” Write the words on the board.
  9. Press 6 — block of code. Three times, only while the answer is True. Thirty seconds; they can see it.
  10. Press 7 — run it. Input 5 gives 6, 7, 8, End. Close on it: “four parts. Three of them you write yourself, and the fourth is the work you actually wanted done.”
  • Seven presses, and this one card replaces what used to be four slides. Do not rush it — it is the centre of the session.
  • Presses 4 and 5 are the whole thing. If you are running late, shorten presses 6 and 7, never the silence on press 4 or the count on press 5.
  • Write initialization, termination condition and updation on the board as they arrive, and leave all three there for the rest of the session.

Watch for A student who says “four” before you count it has understood the back edge already. Ask them to say why, and let the room hear it from them rather than from you.

Concept · 2 min · slide 13 of 27

The shape, with the names on it

How it goes
  1. Straight on from the build: “strip the example away and this is what is left. This is every while loop you will ever write.”
  2. Read it top to bottom against the words already on your board: initialization outside, termination condition on the while line, block of code indented, updation last and still indented.
  3. One check: “which of those four is outside the loop?” Wait for it.
  4. Hand over: “back to the worksheet. I want you to find these three on a program you have not seen.”
  • This is the diagram they will redraw in the exam, and it is the one the takeaways map rebuilds at the end. Leave it up while you give the worksheet task.
  • Sixty seconds. It is a summary, not a new idea.

Watch for Anyone pointing at updation as outside has confused the indent. Fix it here, not later.

Paper 2 · 4 min · slide 14 of 27 · worksheet task 2

Circle the three parts

How it goes
  1. Worksheet task 2. One program, and they have not seen this one.
  2. Read the task off the panel: a line number for each of initialization, termination condition and updation. Say the words in full every time.
  3. Do the first one together, out loud, so nobody is guessing at the task.
  4. Press play. Two and a half minutes. Walk the rows — “any doubts about the task? Ask now” · “one minute, and the question underneath”.
  5. Hands off keyboards first. Then say the check, not before: every loop you will ever write has exactly one of each. If you have typed the same line number twice, look again.
  6. Take the three answers by show of hands, one at a time. Then the number.
  • Press play on the timer.
  • Say the self-check only after they have stopped typing. On the worksheet it would be a hint, not a check.

Watch for The answers are line 2, line 3 and line 6, and line 4 runs five times. The condition uses <= rather than <, which is the bit worth twenty seconds: “counter starts at 1 and the test is less-than-or-equal. Count the yeses.”

Explainer · 4 min · slide 15 of 27

Ask it again — follow the dot

How it goes
  1. Open from the lines they just named: “you found the three lines. Now watch what Python does with them.”
  2. The slider is set to 2. Before you touch anything: “write down what this prints. All of it.” Ten seconds, at the bottom of the worksheet or on scratch paper.
  3. Press Play, then narrate the dot as it moves — one step per sentence, asking rather than telling: “first it sets a to 5, and counter to 0. Now it reaches the diamond. Is 0 less than 2? Yes, so we go inside. a becomes 6. We print a. Now counter becomes 1 …”
  4. Then, with your hand on the back edge: “and look where it goes next. It runs back up that line — to the same question. Is 1 less than 2? Yes again. In we go …”
  5. On the pass that fails, slow right down: “counter is 2. Is 2 less than 2? No. So we do not go in. We come out at the bottom and print End.”
  6. Tie it back to the build: “count the times the dot touched that diamond. Three yeses and a no — four. Same number as before.”
  7. Now drag the slider to 0 and ask before playing: “what prints now?” Take the vote. Most of the room says 6.
  8. Play it. The whole body greys out and stays grey. “Not once. The block ran zero times, and that is not a bug.”
  • One step is the button to use while you narrate — it stops on the line they argued about.
  • Press Reset before you leave the slide, or the next class sees a finished run.

Watch for The 0 prediction is the one that splits the room. Let them be wrong out loud before you play it.

Checkpoint 4 — they can say why a loop never stops

Hands-on · 8 min · slide 16 of 27

Type it, then break it

How it goes
  1. Laptops open. “Type this exactly. Do not copy mine — type it.” The first line asks for a, so run it with a = 5, then a = 1, then a = 100. Four minutes; nine lines with this many people is not one minute.
  2. Walk the rows. Most errors will be the indent or the colon. Say so out loud once so nobody feels singled out.
  3. Step to panel 2 and frame it as your own mistake: “I think I have left a line out by accident — delete counter equals counter plus one and run it again with a = 5.” Do not warn them.
  4. Let them react. Somebody will shout. Only then: “what is it waiting for? Counter is still zero, so the answer to the question never changes.”
  5. Show them the stop button once, then step to panel 3: put the line back, and move it above print(a).
  6. Take the difference from the room: “same three numbers? No — what changed?”
  • Panels 1, 2, 3 step with Next. Each has its own timer; press play on each.
  • Know where the stop button is in your playground before the session.
  • This beat is the fourth common mistake — not updating the counter — and they meet it by doing it, not on a slide. That is why it is not on the sheet later.

Watch for Moving the update above print(a) prints 6, 7, 8 still — counter is not printed. The lesson is that order inside the body matters for what prints, not for how many passes.

Paper 3 · 6 min · slide 17 of 27 · worksheet task 3

Find the mistake

How it goes
  1. Open from what they just did: “you broke updation on purpose. Here are three programs somebody broke by accident.”
  2. Worksheet task 3. Say the shape of it once: three programs, one thing wrong in each, and each is wrong in a different part.
  3. Say the three things they type: which line is wrong — or, if something is simply missing, what is missing and where it should go — then which part it is, then what happens when you run it. Read the inputs off the sheet: 5, then 10, then 1.
  4. Press play. Five minutes. Worksheet tab only — say why: running them answers the question for free.
  5. Walk the rows. “Which part is that? Say the word” · “programme 3 is the sneaky one — count the yeses”.
  6. Hands off keyboards. Do not take the answers here — the next three slides are the answers, one at a time.
  • Press play on the timer.
  • Programme 1 is the one where no line is wrong. That is deliberate, and it is why the task also asks what is missing.
  • Worksheet tab only. If somebody runs programme 2 they freeze a tab, and you lose the reveal.
  • Answers, for you only: programme 1 has no initialization; programme 2 stores the answer of the condition in a box; programme 3 starts the counter at 1 and runs twice, not three times.

Watch for Programme 3 is the one most of them will mark correct. That is the finding, and it is worth saying out loud when the reveal comes.

Reveal 1 of 3 · 4 min · slide 18 of 27

Missing initialization

How it goes
  1. Programme 1 first. Ask for hands before you show anything: “who typed that a line is missing?”
  2. Then reveal, and read the error text out exactly: name counter is not defined.
  3. Ask them to name the part: “which of the three is missing?” Take the word from the room, not from you.
  4. Say the rule they can use in an exam: “if you ever see that error under a while loop, look above the loop, not inside it.”
  5. One extra beat worth eighty seconds: “notice it did not loop forever. It did not loop at all — it stopped before the first pass.”
  • Eighty seconds. It is the easy one of the three.

Watch for Students often read NameError as a typo. Say plainly it is not spelling — the line that creates the variable is simply not there.

Reveal 2 of 3 · 4 min · slide 19 of 27

A condition stored in a box

How it goes
  1. Programme 2. Hands first: “who said line 3? Who said line 4?” Both are defensible — say so.
  2. Read line 3 aloud very slowly: condition equals, bracket, counter less than three, bracket.
  3. Ask the one question and then stop talking: “what gets stored in condition — the question, or the answer?”
  4. Now the slide: the answer, True, stored once. Line 4 asks the box, not the counter. Line 7 keeps changing counter, and nothing ever looks at counter again.
  5. Name it: infinite loop. Then connect it back: “same ending as when you deleted the update line. Different cause.”
  • Ninety seconds. This is the hard one of the three.
  • Call the variable “the box called condition” every time, or the sentence becomes unparseable.

Watch for Two different causes, one symptom. If a student conflates them, ask: “which line was wrong in each?”

Reveal 3 of 3 · 4 min · slide 20 of 27

Off by one

How it goes
  1. Programme 3 last, and ask the honest question: “how many of you marked this one correct?” Count the hands. It will be most of the room.
  2. Point at the output: input is 1, three numbers were wanted, two came out.
  3. Walk the counter out loud with them: starts at 1, so 1 yes, 2 yes, 3 no. Two passes.
  4. Give them both repairs: start at 0 and keep less than 3, or start at 1 and use less than 4. Ask which they prefer and why.
  5. Close the set: “initialization, termination condition, updation. You have now broken all three and seen what each one costs — an error, a wrong count, and a loop that never stops.”
  • This is the mistake that will cost them marks in an exam. Say that out loud.
  • Ninety seconds, and the count of hands at the start is the point of it.

Watch for Anyone who says “just change it to 4” is right and should be asked to say why it works — that is the understanding you want.

Hard floor — exit runs whatever else is dropped

Concept · 2 min · slide 21 of 27

The square pattern

How it goes
  1. Read the question off the slide: stars in N rows and N columns, and for an input of 4 the output is four rows of four.
  2. Point at the output and say the thing that costs marks: there is a space after every star.
  3. Give them the shortcut they already own: “which string operation gives you a star and a space, n times?” Take string repetition from the room.
  4. Ask, and hold the answer: “which part repeats — the row, or the star?” They decide it at the keyboard.
  • Do not code this on screen. They write it.
  • Say the space rule twice. It is the single most common reason a correct program fails a test case here. If nobody recalls string repetition, write "* " * 3 on the board and let them read it.

Watch for A room that says “both” is ahead of the syllabus — tell them they are right, and that the inner one has a shortcut today.

Hands-on · 6 min · slide 22 of 27

Write it in the playground

How it goes
  1. Laptops open, playground open, blank file. Only the input line is on screen; everything after it is theirs.
  2. Before anybody types, take one answer out loud: “n is 3. How many times does the print line have to run?” Three. That is their termination condition, and now they know it.
  3. Press play. Four and a half minutes. Walk the rows continuously — “are you guys done?” · “show me your three parts” · “run it with 5”.
  4. For anyone stuck, ask the three words, do not give code: “where is your initialization?”
  5. Last ninety seconds: everybody runs it with 3, then 5, then 1.
  • Press play on the timer.
  • Nothing goes on the sheet for this one — they type it. The prediction happens out loud before the laptops open.
  • Do not put a solution on screen while they work.

Watch for The common bug is counter less than 3 hard-coded instead of counter less than n — it passes their first test and fails the second. Let them find it with input 5.

Concept · 2 min · slide 23 of 27

The solution

How it goes
  1. Up on screen only after the timer has run out.
  2. Read it against their three words: initialization, termination condition using n, the block, updation.
  3. Point at the line that does the work: print of star-space times n, inside a loop that runs n times. “The star repeats across with the string. The row repeats down with the loop.”
  4. Ask for hands: “who got the same three parts, even if your variable names are different?”

  • A student who stored the row in a variable first is also correct — say so out loud.

Takeaways · 3 min · slide 24 of 27

The map, one press at a time

How it goes
  1. Do not read it out. Ask for it. Press 1 gives them the word loops; then ask “and how many kinds did we see?” before press 2.
  2. Press 2: while and for. One line only — “today was this one. The other one comes in a later session.”
  3. Presses 3 to 6 rebuild the block diagram, one band at a time. Ask for each word before you press: “what goes above the loop? What goes on the while line? What goes at the bottom of the body?”
  4. Press 6 also draws the arrow back up. Point at it: “and that is the bit that surprised you — it asks again.”
  5. Press 7 is three different endings, not one. Take them one at a time: “leave out the initialization — what did programme 1 do?” It stopped with an error. “Get the condition wrong?” Wrong count, or none. “Leave out the updation?” Never stops.
  6. Close: “one question, and then you can go.”
  • Seven presses. Arrow keys work in presenter view.
  • Do not let the last box be read as one sentence. The three parts fail in three different ways, and that distinction is the whole card.

Watch for They should be able to produce all three words unprompted. If they cannot, that is what opens the next session.

Exit · 4 min · slide 25 of 27

One question, and it is hard

How it goes
  1. Frame it as a real thing: “a name-board app prints one letter per line. Somebody types a single letter.”
  2. Read the code once, by line number. Then stop talking and press play on the timer. Two and a half minutes, silent.
  3. Vote by hands, option by option. Count every option out loud.
  4. Reveal. Walk it: length is 1, so the question is 0 less than 0, which is False the first time. The body never runs.
  5. Name each wrong option: 1 is “a loop must run at least once”, 3 is “I saw an index and expected an index error”, 4 is “I saw a while and expected forever”.
  6. Close: “take that same program home and run it with your own name in it.”
  • Press play on the timer. It does not start by itself.
  • This runs even if everything else is cut. It is the hard floor.

Watch for A big vote for option 1 is the finding worth carrying into the next session: they still believe a loop body always runs once.

Common guidelines

Things true of every slide

Never start a slide cold

Every slide opens from something the room already has — the worksheet on their screen, the program they just ran, the answer somebody just gave. If a slide feels abrupt, the fix is one sentence at the end of the previous slide, not a longer explanation on this one.

Walk it in steps, and check as you go

Do not deliver a finished explanation. Say one step, then ask the next as a question — “so counter is 3 now. Is 3 less than 3? No, right? Good.” If the room does not answer, you have found the gap for free. Offer doubts out loud, often; it costs four seconds.

How every poll runs

Let them read and think first. Then count the hands option by option, out loud, including the options nobody picked. Say once, at the start of the session, that a closed fist means “not sure” and that it is a real answer you want to see. Reveal. Then explain every option, not just the right one — the wrong ones are where the misconceptions live.

Picking students

Call a row or a side of the room, never a name. Rotate which group you call. Never name a student who got something wrong, and never say whose screen you are looking at.

When nobody answers

Count ten seconds in your head. Then narrow the question rather than answering it: “point at the line that repeats” is answerable when “what do you notice?” is not.

Why they commit before they run

It was never really about paper — it is that a student has to commit to an answer before the machine hands them one. The worksheet is where they commit; the playground is where they find out. A student who predicts and then runs it discovers they were wrong; a student who runs it first discovers nothing, because the machine answered before they had a position. So: worksheet tab only on a worksheet beat, and the playground shut. The one beat that has no worksheet task is the square pattern, and there the prediction is taken out loud before any playground opens.

Say the three words in full, every time

Initialization, termination condition, updation. Not “the first bit”, not “the check”, not “the plus one”. They are named once, properly, on the build card, and everything after that — the sheet, the mistake hunt, the map — assumes the room has the words. Write all three on the board as they arrive and leave them there.

The counts are the understanding

For the program on the build card, initialization runs once, the condition is asked four times, and the block and the updation run three times each. The four is the number that tells you whether a student has understood the back edge. Ask for it more than once: on press 5 of the build, again on the dot, again if it comes up on the sheet.

Two words that mean different things today

Say “the termination condition” for the test on the while line, and “the counter” for the variable. Never say “the value” for either. In programme 2 of the mistake hunt there is also a variable named condition — call that one “the box called condition” every time, or the sentence becomes unparseable.

Reading the room

Still typing after the timer means the task was too long — take the next one down by a minute. A room on the worksheet tab and nothing else is the beat working. If they ask

  • “Can I use a for loop instead?” Not today. The for loop has its own session; today the point is the three lines you have to write yourself.
  • “Which is better, while or for?” Neither. You pick by what you are counting, and you will be able to choose once you have both.
  • “Does the counter have to be called counter?” No. Call it i, or count, or anything. The name has no meaning to Python.
  • “Can the counter go down instead of up?” Yes — start at 3 and use counter > 0 with counter = counter - 1. Same three parts.
  • “What if the condition is never False?” The program runs until you stop it. That is the infinite loop, and it is not an error message — nothing is wrong as far as Python is concerned.
  • “Why is print("End") not indented?” Same rule as yesterday: the indent says which block a line belongs to. Outdented means it is outside the loop, so it runs once, after.
  • “My infinite loop froze the playground.” Stop button beside Run. If the tab is unresponsive, reload it — the code is saved.
