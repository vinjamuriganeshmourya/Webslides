Session 11 · Nested Conditional Statements · 75 minutes · read this once before class, keep it open on your laptop during

How to use this

One entry per slide, in the order you will present them. How it goes is the thread of the slide — what to say, in order. Quoted phrases are worth saying close to as written; the rest is yours, in your own words.

Nothing starts out of nowhere. Every slide begins from something the room already has — an app they use, the program they just ran, the answer they just gave — and ends by handing over to the next slide. If you find yourself opening a slide cold, go back one step and connect it.

Three habits carry the session. Walk it in small steps and check as you go — “so what happens if the PIN is wrong? Will it even look at the balance? No, right?” Ask before you tell, always. And offer doubts often: “any doubts here?” costs four seconds and saves ten minutes.

Hook · 3 min · slide 5 of 31 · three presses

Behind the scenes on a ₹450 payment

How it goes
  1. Start from what everyone has done: “I think all of us have used GPay or PhonePe to send money to someone.”
  2. “Let us see what actually happens behind the scenes.” Walk the order: it checks the PIN first, then the balance, and only then does the money move.
  3. Check them with a question, do not tell: “so what if the PIN is wrong? Will it even check your bank balance? No, right? Good.”
  4. Press once. The gate shuts and the balance check greys out on its own. Say nothing while it moves, then: “nobody ever found out whether you had the money.”
  5. Press again: “now let us see how this looks in code. Two ifs, one inside the other. PIN okay, go inside and check balance. Balance okay, then only send.”
  6. Offer doubts, then hand off: “any doubts here? Good — now you tell me if this sort of thing has happened to you this week.”
  • Right arrow steps the animation. r replays it.
  • Call it “an if inside an if”, never “two ifs” on their own.

Watch for anyone saying “two separate ifs”. That phrase becomes the wrong shape three slides from now — correct it here, gently.

Your turn · 1 min · slide 6 of 31

Your turn — where else did that happen?

How it goes
  1. Carry the question straight over from the payment: “that app asked the second question only because the first one passed. Where else has that happened to you this week?”
  2. Then stop talking. The silence is doing the work — do not fill it with your own example.
  3. Take four. Repeat each one back in their words and write it on the board.
  4. Do not judge any of them. “Interesting” is enough for all four.
  5. Hand off by picking one: “someone said movie tickets — let us actually look at that one.”
  • If the room is truly silent after ten seconds, name something on this campus — the WiFi login, the canteen slide, the gate register — and ask who else has one.
  • Never go round naming people.

Watch for answers that describe two separate checks rather than one inside another. Leave it — the next slide settles it. Keep the four on the board; they come back at the takeaways.

The nested shape · 3 min · slide 9 of 31

Nested Conditions — the shape and its three names

How it goes
  1. Open with the question, not the answer: “four blocks on the screen. Which of these depends on condition A?” Take answers.
  2. Then confirm and extend: “if A is false, Python jumps the whole orange box — without reading a single line inside it.”
  3. Name the parts as you point: the orange box is the conditional block; Block 2, indented under the inner if, is the nested conditional block.
  4. Check them: “how many yeses does Block 2 need? And Block 3? And Block 4?” Write 2, 1, 0 on the board as they answer.
  5. Any doubts, then hand off: “let us trace one of these ourselves.”
  • Do not open the worksheet yet. It opens on the next slide.

Watch for the 2, 1, 0 answers. If they come easily, the hook did its job.

Hands-on 1 · 7 min · slide 10 of 31 · worksheet page 1, task 1

Trace it first, then run it

How it goes
  1. Send them to the worksheet now, while they are looking at the program: “open the session worksheet on your laptop, name and roll number at the top — remember, this comes back to me at the end.”
  2. Set it up from the diagram they just saw: “this is that same shape, as a real program. Nine lines.”
  3. “Do not run it yet — I want it traced in the worksheet first. All four combinations of A and B, what does each one print. Page one, task one. Five minutes.” Start the timer.
  4. While they work, walk the rows and check in — spread these out, one at a time: “are you guys done?” · “come on, let us see who finishes first” · “any doubts about the task? Ask now” · “two minutes left, finish the last row.”
  5. Hands off keyboards, then ask — do not tell: “which two rows printed exactly the same thing?” Wait. “And what does that tell you about condition B?”
  6. Hand off to the keyboard: “now type it in and run all four. Change only line 1 and line 2 — the machine is just checking whether you were right.”
  • Worksheet open and named before you start the timer.
  • Press play on the timer. It does not start by itself.
  • Short of time: trace all four in the worksheet, run only rows 3 and 4.

Watch for print("Block 2") indented twice on their screens. Answers: True/True → 1, 2, 3, 4 · True/False → 1, 3, 4 · False/either → 4 only.

Hands-on 2 · 5 min · slide 11 of 31 · two steps

Nesting inside else

How it goes
  1. Connect to the program still on their screens: “almost the same program — one thing has moved. What is different?” Take hands until someone says the inner if went into the else.
  2. “Right. So now the second question gets asked only when the first one says no.”
  3. Type it together: “I am typing it too, keep pace with me.” Before every run: “predict it first — what will it print?”
  4. Let them play: “now change both names however you like, and tell me which blocks never printed for you.”
  5. Any doubts, then hand off: “good. Now a bigger one — a program that claims to do something. Let us see whether it actually does.”
  • Type on the projector at their pace, not yours.

Watch for the point landing: when condition_a is True the else is skipped entirely, so condition_b is never read. Answers: a False b True → 2, 3, 4 · a False b False → 3, 4 · a True, either b → 1, 4.

Activity · 8 min · slide 12 of 31 · worksheet page 2, task 2

Program tracing — does it do what it claims?

How it goes
  1. Frame it as a claim to test: “this one says it prints the greatest of three numbers. Page two, task two — trace it and tell me whether it really does.”
  2. Model the first two lines aloud — say what the machine does before what the line means — then: “the rest is yours.”
  3. While they work: “are you guys done?” · “any doubts on the task, ask now” · “fill a row only if that line actually runs.”
  4. Hands off keyboards, and only then the check: “exactly two of the twelve rows should be empty. Does yours match?” Then take the verdict: “so does it work?”
  5. Now break it: “type it with the last four lines pulled out to column zero, and run 1 2 9, then 5 2 1.”
  6. Hand off with the question, unanswered: “why does one input error and the other one not? Hold that — next slide is that exact program.”
  • Do not give the two-empty-rows check before hands are off keyboards.
  • Short of time: skip the pairing, keep both runs.

Watch for the verdict: yes, it prints 9. Broken version: 5 2 1 prints 5 then NameError; 1 2 9 prints 9 cleanly. Same program, right for one input and dead for the other — and they built it, so nobody can call it a trick.

Type it and run it · 2 min · slide 13 of 31

Nested Conditions — the correct program

How it goes
  1. Show them the clean version first: “this is the correct one — same program as your worksheet, every branch properly indented.”
  2. Break it live: “watch what I change — nothing but where these four lines sit.” Pull if is_b_greatest: down to print(c) out to column zero.
  3. Ask, do not tell: “so why did 5 2 1 die and 1 2 9 not?” Wait for them.
  4. Then say it: “is_b_greatest is created inside the else. When the if branch runs, that name never exists.”

Stand-up · 2 min · slide 15 of 31 · the one physical activity

You are the program

How it goes
  1. Pick up the promise you made before the quiz: “nobody types this one. You are the program.”
  2. Read all three lines out before anyone moves — one at a time teaches a sequence, not a nest.
  3. Walk the moves aloud: “line one is a question, answer it in your head. Yes means stand. If you are standing, now answer line three — hand up if your roll number ends in an even digit. Last line is at column zero, so everybody sits.” Then: “go.”
  4. Now the payoff, to the half still sitting: “everyone who stayed seated — hands up if you checked your roll number.” No hands.
  5. Land it: “that is the whole idea. You did not check it and find it false. You never read that line at all.”

Discover · 5 min · slide 16 of 31 · four steps

Discover it first — how would you grade this?

How it goes
  1. Set the problem plainly: “marks out of 100, print the grade. Four bands, on the screen.”
  2. Hand it over with a constraint: “write it with what you already know — there is no new keyword yet. Three minutes, on your own screen.”

elif · 4 min · slide 17 of 31

Elif Statement — the skeleton

How it goes
  1. Answer the question you just left hanging: “that one word is elif.”
  2. Define it in their terms: “elif means — if the previous question was false, here is the next one.”
  3. Point at the indents: “notice there is no box inside a box. All three questions sit at the same level. This is a ladder, not a nest.”
  4. Any doubts, then hand off: “now let us put it on your grade problem.”

elif · slide 18 of 31

Multiple Elif Statements — the grade ladder

How it goes
  1. Give them ownership: “this is your problem, solved. Same four bands, one check each.”
  2. Ask before explaining: “marks are 72 — what prints?” Take the answer from them.
  3. Then extend it: “C. And notice — three conditions were tested, but the 61 check and the else were never even looked at.”
  4. Two facts worth saying here: “you can chain as many elifs as you like, and the final else is optional — leave it off and 40 marks prints nothing at all.”

  6. Hand off: “let us actually watch one of these run, line by line.”

Follow the dot · 2 min · slide 19 of 31

How elif actually runs

How it goes
  1. Set up a disagreement first: “marks are 72. How many conditions does Python actually test? Hands for one … two … three … four.”
  2. Let them argue for a moment, then vote again. “Okay — let us watch it.”
  3. Press play once and say nothing while the dot moves.
  4. Then read the result off the screen: “three tested, one printed. Look at the grey — two checks and the else were never looked at. Not read and found false. Never read.”
  5. Try two more: drag to 95 — one test, everything below it grey. Drag to 40 — four tests, all false, and only then the else.
  6. Hand off: “now you type one of these yourself.”

Hands-on · 4 min · slide 20 of 31 · three steps

Add one more grade

How it goes
  1. “Type the ladder from the slide and run a few different marks. Two minutes.” Check in while they type: “are you guys done? Any doubts?”
  2. Then spring it, with no warning: “I forgot grade E, for 51 to 60. Add it at the bottom, after the else. Run it. What happened?”
  3. Let them react before you explain. Then read the error out: “SyntaxError: 'elif' block follows an 'else' block — and notice, nothing printed. Not even the grades that worked a minute ago.”
  4. Say why: “Python read the whole file first, found a check after the catch-all, and refused to start.”
  5. Ask before showing: “so where does E belong?” Then show the correct ladder and let them fix their own.
  6. Hand off: “keep that program open. Pens for two minutes.”
  • Do not warn them before the broken version. The surprise is the lesson.
  • Short of time: move E yourself.

Watch for the surprise that nothing prints. Fix: a fourth elif marks >= 51 above the else; then 95, 85, 72, 65, 55, 40 give A, B, C, D, E, F.

Common Mistake — elif after else

Common guidelines

Never start a slide cold

Every slide should open from something the room already has — an app they use, the program still on their screens, the answer somebody just gave — and close by pointing at the next one. If a slide ever feels abrupt, the fix is almost always one sentence at the end of the previous slide, not a longer explanation on this one.

Walk it in steps, and check as you go

Do not deliver a finished explanation. Say one step, then ask the next one as a question: “so if the PIN is wrong, will it even check the balance? No, right? Good.” The “right?” is not filler — it is a checkpoint, and if the room does not answer it, you have found the gap before it cost you anything. Offer doubts at the end of every concept slide: four seconds, and it saves ten minutes later.

How every poll runs

Same three moves each time. Let them think — the timer is thinking time, not pressure. Count all four options out loud before you reveal anything; once they know the answer you can no longer see what they actually thought. Then reveal and say a line about every option, wrong ones included — a wrong option nobody explains is a wrong option they keep.

Picking students — the rotation rule

Never call a name. Call a group, so nobody is singled out and nobody can hide: roll numbers ending 1, 2 or 3 · first names starting with a vowel · the window row · everyone who stayed seated in the stand-up. Different group every question, different sets next session. If the same four hands answer everything, you have a quiet class and a loud four, not an engaged class.

When nobody answers

Count ten seconds in your head before you fill the silence. It almost never takes ten. If it does, narrow the question — “is it the first line or the second one?” — rather than answering it yourself.

Why tracing before running

Running code is fast and forgiving: a wrong guess costs nothing, because the machine answers in half a second. A trace has no machine to ask, so the only way to fill the row is to run the program in your own head — which is the skill the whole session is building. The run afterwards is not the exercise; it is the marking scheme. Skip the trace and the room still gets the right answers, just from the interpreter rather than from themselves.

Reading the room

  • Fewer than half the hands up on a poll — they did not understand the question, not the topic. Read the question again; do not explain the answer.
  • The same four voices every time — rotation rule, immediately.
  • Heads down and no typing during a hands-on — the instruction was unclear, not the task. Say it again in one sentence.
  • Nobody asks a doubt when you offer — walk the rows instead. The doubts come one to one, not to the whole room.
  • They finish a trace in under two minutes — they guessed. Ask one of them to read row 3 aloud.

The dash on the worksheet

A dash in a trace table means this name does not have a value yet. It is a correct answer, not a blank. Say that when the worksheet opens, or half the room will leave those boxes empty and assume they failed the task.

Why the worksheet repeats itself

Task 1 and task 3 ask nearly the same thing, and that is deliberate. The second one is not revision — it is the check that the first one stuck, and the twenty minutes of new material between them is the point. Do not let anyone skip task 3 because it looks familiar.
